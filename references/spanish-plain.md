# Plain Spanish ("español plano")

When the reader wants Spanish, write the way a smart friend explains a book over coffee: plain Spanish that reads without effort anywhere in Latin America. The goal is zero friction. If a word makes the reader pause, swap it.

By default, write **neutral Latin American Spanish**. If the reader names a country or region ("como se habla en República Dominicana", "español de México"), add that regional flavor using the section further down. Ask once during setup if they haven't said.

## Simple, neutral Spanish (default)

- Use **tú** for the reader and **ustedes** for plural. Never *vosotros*, never *vos*, unless the reader asks for Spain or Río de la Plata Spanish.
- **No slang** from any country (*vale*, *tío*, *güey*, *chévere*, *pana*, *vaina*…).
- Pick everyday words over formal ones:
  - *usar*, not *utilizar*
  - *empezar*, not *iniciar* or *comenzar a implementar*
  - *mostrar*, not *evidenciar*
  - *por eso*, not *por consiguiente* or *en consecuencia*
  - *hacer*, not *llevar a cabo*
  - *cambiar*, not *modificar*
  - *ayudar*, not *coadyuvar*
  - *importante*, not *primordial*, *fundamental* or *crucial*
  - *mientras más X, menos Y*, not *X es inversamente proporcional a Y*
  - *lo curioso es que*, not *paradójicamente*
- Math, academic, or legal phrasing in the original ("inversely proportional", "a priori") gets rewritten as everyday Spanish, even when it's the author's axiom. Keep the idea; drop the jargon.
- Short sentences. If one sentence has two commas and a "que", split it.
- Active voice: "La gente nota tu ansiedad", not "Tu ansiedad es percibida por la gente".
- Keep full orthography: accents, ñ, ¿ ¡. Plain doesn't mean careless.

## Spanglish, on purpose

Spanglish is on by default; drop it if the reader asks for pure Spanish. Use it when translating would lose the meaning, the punch, or the joke, not just to sound cool:

- **The author's terms of art** stay in English, with a quick plain gloss the first time: "neediness (necesitar la aprobación de otros)". After that, use the English term alone.
- **Jokes and comebacks** that only work in English. Keep the English line and, if needed, add a short Spanish follow-up.
- **Pop-culture or industry words** Spanish speakers already use in English: *pickup*, *date*, *ghosting*, *gym*, *season tickets*. If the Spanish version sounds stiff or loses the cultural picture ("abono para la temporada"), keep the English.
- **Idioms and phrasal verbs whose literal translation kills the meaning**: keep the English. "Un comentario inofensivo la puede *turn off*", not "apagarla". Same for *turn on*, *friend zone*, *hit on*, *make a move*, *play it cool*, *be into someone*. Test: if a bilingual Latin American would say it in English in conversation, keep it in English.
- **Profanity**: match the author's level. Use common Spanish ("mierda", "carajo") or leave the English ("fuck", "bullshit") when it lands harder. Don't sanitize and don't add more.

## Regional flavor (optional)

When the reader asks for a country's way of speaking, change how sentences are **built** and which **everyday words** you choose, not the vocabulary of slang. Every line should still read clearly for any Spanish speaker. Use local profanity choices (for example, avoid Spain's "joder" outside Spain).

### Example: Dominican Republic

- **Say the subject pronoun out loud**: "en cómo tú te paras", "yo sé que eso suena frío", "a mí me gusta cómo él se maneja".
- **Questions without inversion**: "¿Qué tú crees?", "¿Quién tú crees que ella iba a escoger?", not "¿Qué crees tú?".
- **Spoken connectors**: "Lo que pasa es que…", "de eso es que estamos hablando", "y ya", "lo más que…", "entonces", "fíjate".
- **Everyday words**: *carro*, *manejar*, *escoger*, *echar chistes*, *bajarle la cabeza a alguien*, *no anda buscando*, *bregar con*.
- **Still no slang**: no *vaina*, *pariguayo*, *jeva*, *un chin*, *tíguere*, *jevi*, *chércha*.
- Profanity that sounds natural there: "mierda", "carajo", "coño" (sparingly).

A reader-approved sample of this tone:

> Lo que pasa es que la neediness es lo que mueve todo lo que tú haces, y por eso se mete en todo: en cómo tú te paras, en cómo sonríes, en los chistes que haces, en el carro que manejas. […] Los dos pueden comprarse los season tickets del equipo o estar echando chistes. La diferencia está en la *intención*. Uno lo hace para que lo aprueben. El otro lo hace porque le gusta, y ya.
>
> Sí, yo sé que eso suena frío. A mí también me incomodó cuando me di cuenta. Pero piénsalo. […] Entonces, ¿qué tú crees que miraba una mujer? Cómo él se comportaba.

For another country, build the same short list (pronoun habits, question order, connectors, everyday words, slang to avoid) before writing, and apply it lightly: a Dominican, Mexican, or Colombian should recognize the rhythm, but nobody should have to look up a word.

## Same voice, different language

Everything in `anti-ai-voice.md` still applies. Write *as* the author (first person, talking to "tú"), keep their order of argument, their stories with names and numbers, and their attitude. Adapt; don't translate sentence by sentence. A translated paragraph that reads stiff is worse than a freer one that sounds like the author.

Also avoid the Spanish AI tells: "En este capítulo…", "Cabe destacar", "Es importante señalar", "En definitiva", "adentrarnos", "un viaje", "desbloquear", "clave" in every other line.

## Quick test

Read one paragraph out loud. Would a 16-year-old get it on the first read? Does it still sound like the author talking? If either answer is no, simplify or loosen it.
