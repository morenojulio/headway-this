# Writing so it doesn't read like AI

The reader should feel they're reading a tighter version of the author's own book. These are the habits that break that illusion, and what to do instead.

## The biggest one: no narrator

Don't write *about* the book ("In this chapter, the author argues…", "Carnegie explains that…", "En este capítulo, el autor nos muestra…"). That third-person frame is the clearest sign a summary was generated. Write *as* the author would if they had to say the same thing in a fifth of the space. If the author writes in first person, use first person. If they talk to "you", talk to "you".

The exception is when the author's voice depends on a story only they lived ("When I was fired from…"). Keep the first person and tell it shorter. Don't invent anecdotes they didn't tell.

## Phrases and moves to cut

- Openers that announce: "In today's fast-paced world", "Have you ever wondered…", "Imagine a world where…", "Let's dive in", "En el mundo actual…", "¿Alguna vez te has preguntado…?"
- Closers that wrap up: "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "At the end of the day", "In summary", "En definitiva", "En resumen".
- Hedging filler: "It's important to note", "It's worth mentioning", "Cabe destacar", "Es importante señalar".
- AI-typical vocabulary: delve, tapestry, landscape, realm, navigate (figurative), unlock, harness, journey (figurative), game-changer, pivotal, crucial, profundizar, adentrarnos, un viaje, desbloquear, clave (when overused).
- Reflex triads: "clear, concise, and compelling". Use three items only when the author actually lists three.
- Too many "It's not X, it's Y" lines. Use it once if the author does.
- Em-dash chains. Match how often the author uses dashes; most authors use far fewer than models do.
- Bolded mini-headings every two paragraphs inside the prose. The summary is continuous prose, as in a book chapter.
- Every paragraph the same length and shape. Authors vary; mix short punchy paragraphs with longer ones the way the author does.
- A rhetorical question to open every section.

## Things that make it read as the author

- Their **terms of art**, kept exactly ("Deep Work", "the 1% rule", "habit stacking"). Never swap them for synonyms.
- Their **examples and stories**, compressed but concrete: names, numbers, places. Specifics are what generic summaries lose first.
- Their **rhythm**: if Bennett writes long sentences with ironic asides, so do you. If Godin writes three-line paragraphs, so do you.
- Their **attitude**: provocative, warm, clinical, funny, scolding. Keep it, including opinions you might soften.
- Their **order of argument**. If they start with a story and reveal the principle later, do the same; don't move the thesis to the top.

## Quick test before saving a chapter

Read the first and last paragraph. Would a fan of the author find either one off? Is there a sentence that could appear in a summary of *any* book? Rewrite it with something specific to this one.
